Time travel would be useless; you could go back 100 times and fail every single time.

There's a theory in physics called the Novikov self-consistency principle. Basically it says that if you travelled into the past, the universe wouldn't allow you to create a paradox. Imagine going back to stop your parents from meeting: you miss the train, you get the date wrong, they meet somewhere else by accident, or it gets even stranger, and your attempt to keep them apart becomes the reason they meet in the first place.

According to Novikov, you could visit the past, you could participate in it, you just couldn't change it. Because whatever you did there was already part of history.

Novikov developed this idea while studying Einstein's theory of general relativity, and Einstein's equations actually allow for structures called closed timelike curves, loops in spacetime where the future bends back and reconnects with the past. The mathematics is real. The geometry is allowed. Technically, the universe permits it.

But this is where Novikov's principle gets really strange. You wouldn't have a one-in-a-billion chance of creating a paradox. The probability would be absolute zero.

So imagine travelling back to 1920 to prevent a famous fire. You find the building. You warn everyone. You rush inside to help. And in the chaos, you bump into someone carrying an oil lamp. The lamp shatters, and the flames spread. You didn't prevent a fire, you caused it. There was never another version of history. It was always you.

That's the strange part. Inside a loop like that, what would free will even mean? You could affect the past, because your actions happened there. But you couldn't change it, because those actions were already part of it.

You thought you were going back to rewrite history, but you were actually going back to complete it. You were never going to change the past. The past was waiting for you.

Did you get it?

1

You go back to stop your parents from ever meeting. What happens?

Something always goes wrong: a missed train, a wrong date. Or, stranger still, the attempt to separate them turns out to be the reason they met.
2

What are the odds of creating a paradox? Why does that exact number matter?

Zero, not one in a billion. A tiny chance would still let paradoxes happen eventually; zero rules them out completely.
3

Who started the 1920 fire?

The traveller who came back to prevent it. And it had always been him: there was never a version of history without him in it.

Four ways out

Other theories, 20 minutes

Novikov's answer is only one of several, and the alternatives are just as strange.

a loop a branch a conjecture to rule something out the origin of something

The self-consistency principle

Igor Novikov, Russian astrophysicist, mid-1980s

The one from the video, and the one everything else in this lesson argues with. You can go back, walk around, touch things. You simply cannot produce a contradiction. Anything you do in the past has already happened. You are not an intruder in history, you are a line in it that was always there.

If you were a fixed point on a line drawn before you were born, would that feel like comfort or like a prison?

Many worlds, many timelines

Hugh Everett, applied to time travel by David Deutsch

The exact opposite. Every time you change something in the past, reality splits and you continue in a new branch. So you can stop the fire, but not the fire in the history you came from. That one still burned. You saved a city in a world you can never get back to, and the people you were trying to help are still gone in yours.

Does it count as saving someone if you save them in a branch you'll never return from? Would you still do it?

Chronology protection

Stephen Hawking, 1992

Hawking's answer was blunter: the laws of physics are arranged so that a time machine can never be built in the first place. His favourite argument wasn't an equation, it was a question. If time travel were ever going to be invented, where are the tourists from the future? In 2009 he threw a party for time travellers and sent the invitations out afterwards. Nobody came.

Is the empty party good evidence, or just a good joke? What would a visitor from 2300 have to gain by showing up?

The bootstrap paradox

Not a way out, a new problem

Novikov's loops allow something odd. Suppose you take a symphony back to the composer before he writes it. He copies it down, it becomes famous, you learn it, you take it back. The music exists, it is performed, people cry at it, and nobody ever wrote it. The same works for objects, ideas, even for a person's own existence.

Who wrote that symphony? Does a thing need an origin in order to exist, or is existing enough?
